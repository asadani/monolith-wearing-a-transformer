A Monolith Wearing a Transformer

What we un-learned about decoupling.

A domain foundation model is a strong supporting system and a poor primary one. Not because the model is bad. Because of what you can reason about at three in the morning when it is quietly wrong across four decisions at once.

01What set this off

A research document proposed that routing, fraud, risk and checkout all be served from a single payments foundation model. It specified the model's inference budget at 29 milliseconds, four times over, each instance carrying a citation marker.

The number appears in none of the vendor's own materials — not the product page, the engineering blog, the launch release, or the CEO's newspaper interview. Nobody lied. A model produced a document about a real system and a plausible latency landed in a slot that wanted one.

The failure is not fabrication as an act. It is fabrication as a shape: a document that looks cited, reads as technical, and carries one load-bearing quantity that came from nowhere.

02What service boundaries were actually for

Not development velocity — that was the conference pitch, and for many teams it never arrived. The durable benefit was containment: when the fraud scorer degrades, checkout still completes.

The pattern has a name. A bulkhead isolates parts of a system into compartments "so that failure of one component will not cascade to other components." It does not prevent failure. It bounds it.

A shared backbone serving all four decisions removes that compartment. Its failure becomes a single point of failure by construction rather than by misconfiguration — and that distinction is the whole argument. A bug gets fixed and the architecture is unchanged. A design working as intended has no configuration that restores the boundary.

Redundancy does not help, because the failure mode that matters is not the model being down. It is the model being wrong. Five replicas of a drifted model produce the same drifted answer five times, quickly.

03What the evidence actually shows

Somebody tried it. Adyen reports attempting bigger deep learning models combining multiple decisions, and finding they would "compromise the engineering requirements (latency, uptime) of online deployments in a critical flow." What failed was not accuracy. It was latency and uptime, in the synchronous path.

And they intend to try again — the same source says they expect to move the whole pipeline to deep learning "in the short future." Both halves belong in any honest reading of it.

Nobody has benchmarked the actual question. No published comparison of a consolidated backbone against task-specific models on production payment traffic exists, on either side. The tabular literature reached for instead scopes itself to ~10K samples; these systems train on four billion payments. It neither supports nor refutes them.

What vendors do publish — Stripe's card-testing detection going from 59% to 97%, Plaid's numbers at fixed operating points — are all capability results. Not one is a containment result. None reports what happened when the model was wrong, or how long attribution took.

04What to do instead

Put it beside the path, not in it. The highest-value position is a representation layer: the model produces embeddings, and task-specific classifiers consume them and decide. This is the shape Stripe describes. The compartment survives, because the shared thing produces inputs rather than verdicts.

Five conditions before promoting anything into the critical path:

  1. A fallback that is exercised, not documented. Running on live traffic continuously — an untested fallback is a comment in a runbook.
  2. Per-decision observability. Aggregate model health hides one decision degrading while the average holds.
  3. A measured mean time to attribution. Induce a degradation, time how long the on-call takes to find it. Almost nobody has this number.
  4. Independent rollback. Revert one decision path without reverting the others — usually versioned embeddings with pinning.
  5. Regional and segment validation before scale, not after.

The burden of proof runs this way because the errors are not the same size. A supporting system that turns out excellent costs a slower rollout. A primary decision-maker that turns out subtly wrong costs money moving incorrectly, as fast as traffic arrives, across four decision types at once.

What this argument is not

The containment reasoning is deduction, not measurement, and it is marked low confidence in the paper's ledger for exactly that reason. The architectural evidence is largely vendor-published. If someone runs the comparison and it goes the other way, this was a well-sourced argument for the wrong conclusion.

The full paper names, in advance, the five findings that would change its mind — and the one kind of result that would not.
